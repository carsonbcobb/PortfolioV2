**By Carson Cobb. Data collected November 16, 2026.**

Shipping times, return windows, a way to reach the brand, a way to hear when a sold out product is back, and the one line of text at the top of every page. These are the details shoppers check before they buy, and the ones AI shopping agents now read for them.

I checked all five on the same 107 Shopify brand stores, 17 to 18 in each of six product areas, on November 16, 2026. This report brings the five checks together, one section each.

## The short version

- **Only half of shipping policies give a delivery range.** 49 of 95 (52%) stated a range of days, and 7% stated an order cutoff.
- **30 days is the default return window.** 50 of 77 policies that stated a window (65%) used exactly 30, and only 11% offered free returns.
- **32% of homepages offer no chat, email or phone.** 60% load a chat widget, 24% link an email address and 12% a phone number.
- **36% of sold out pages capture nothing.** 28 of 77 sold out product pages had no back in stock signup.
- **Announcement bars all say the same thing.** 56% promote free shipping. Only 2 of 63 mention returns.
- **The banner and the policy often disagree.** 33 stores advertised a free shipping threshold in their announcement bar. Only 12 of their shipping policies stated the same threshold.

## Shipping policies

"When will it get here?" and "How much is shipping?" are two of the questions that decide a sale. Shoppers ask them on the product page, in the cart, in chat, and now they ask AI. I read the published shipping policy of all 107 stores and compared it with the free shipping offer in each store's announcement bar. 95 had a readable shipping policy.

- **12 of 107 stores had no readable shipping policy.** That's 11%.
- **Only half give a time range.** 49 of 95 policies (52%) stated a range of days.
- **Order cutoffs are almost never stated.** 7 of 95 (7%).
- **Tracking is mentioned in only 36%.** 34 of 95.

| Policy element | Stores | Share of 95 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Processing or handling time | 57 | 60% |
| International shipping mentioned | 52 | 55% |
| A time range in days | 49 | 52% |
| Tracking mentioned | 34 | 36% |
| Free shipping threshold stated | 25 | 26% |
| Daily order cutoff time | 7 | 7% |

The gap that matters most is the cutoff. A shopper buying a gift on a Tuesday wants to know whether ordering by 2pm means it ships today. 93% of policies don't say, so the shopper either guesses or leaves to check a competitor who does. The average documented cart abandonment rate is 70.22% across 50 studies ([cart abandonment statistics](/reports/cart-abandonment-statistics-2026)).

Of the 49 policies with a time range, the upper end of the first range stated was 5 days or less in 30 of them. Some of those ranges describe processing time, not delivery, and many policies never make clear which is which. "Ships in 1 to 3 business days" and "arrives in 1 to 3 business days" are very different promises.

### The banner says one thing, the policy another

Free shipping thresholds are the most common message in Shopify announcement bars (see [announcement bars](#announcement-bars) below). But the policy page often doesn't back it up.

| Stores advertising a free shipping threshold in the announcement bar | 33 |
| --- | --- |
| Policy states the same threshold | 12 |
| Policy doesn't state the threshold | 21 |

I found no store where the two numbers conflicted. The problem is silence. When the policy doesn't mention the offer, the shopper who checks the policy before buying, and the AI agent that reads it, can't confirm it. Among the 25 policies that did state a threshold, the median was $59.

### What to do with your shipping policy

1. **State processing and delivery separately.** "Orders ship within 1 business day. Standard delivery takes 3 to 5 business days."
2. **Add a cutoff time.** "Orders placed by 2pm Pacific ship the same day." Only 7% of stores do this, so it's an easy edge.
3. **Put your free shipping threshold in the policy.** Use the exact number from your banner. Set it with the [free shipping threshold calculator](/tools/free-shipping-calculator).
4. **List your shipping costs.** A small table of methods, prices and times answers the question before it's asked.
5. **Show the delivery estimate on the product page and in the cart.** The policy page is the source of truth, but shoppers decide earlier.
6. **Keep it current.** If you change carriers, thresholds or holiday cutoffs, update the policy the same day.

## Return policies

Your return policy does two jobs. It's a reason to buy for the shopper who's on the fence, and it's a promise your support team has to keep. Most stores write it once, copy a template, and never look at it again. I read the published return policy of all 107 stores. 101 had a readable policy.

- **30 days is the default.** Of 77 policies that stated a window in days, 50 (65%) used exactly 30.
- **Only 16 went longer than 30 days.** 11 offered less.
- **Free returns are rare.** 11 of 101 policies (11%) mentioned free returns or a prepaid label.
- **Half list things you can't return.** 51 of 101 (50%) named final sale or non returnable items.
- **24 of 101 policies never state a window in days.** The shopper has to guess or ask.
- **6 of 107 stores had no readable return policy at all.**

| Return window stated in the policy | Stores | Share of 77 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 14 days or less | 8 | 10% |
| 15 to 29 days | 3 | 4% |
| Exactly 30 days | 50 | 65% |
| 31 to 60 days | 10 | 13% |
| More than 60 days | 6 | 8% |

The median was 30 days. Supplements and home goods had the most stores going past 30, usually framed as a guarantee ("try it for 60 days"). Food stores were the least likely to state any window, because most don't accept returns on perishable goods. When 65% of stores say the same thing, 30 days stops being a reason to buy. A longer window, or a guarantee written in plain words, is one of the cheapest ways to stand out, and it's easy to test.

| Policy element | Stores | Share of 101 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exchanges mentioned | 67 | 66% |
| Final sale or non returnable items | 51 | 50% |
| Guarantee language (money back or satisfaction) | 44 | 44% |
| Conditions on opened or used products | 41 | 41% |
| States a window in days | 77 | 76% |
| Store credit mentioned | 18 | 18% |
| Free returns or prepaid return label | 11 | 11% |
| Restocking fee | 9 | 9% |

Two things stand out. First, 44 stores use guarantee language, but only 11 make returns free. A guarantee the shopper pays to use is a weaker promise than it sounds. Second, the opened product rule is where most support tickets start. In supplements and beauty, "unopened only" means most unhappy customers can't return anything, and the policy should say so before they buy, not after.

### What to do with your return policy

1. **Put the window in the first sentence.** "You have 30 days to return any unopened product" beats three paragraphs of conditions.
2. **Say what can't be returned, up front.** If opened products or sale items are final, list them in plain words, and show the same rule on those product pages.
3. **Consider going past 30 days.** If your return rate is low, a 60 day window or a guarantee costs little and gives shoppers a reason to choose you over the 65% who offer 30.
4. **If you use the word guarantee, make it easy to use.** A prepaid label or a refund without a return turns a claim into a real promise.
5. **Use the same numbers everywhere.** Your policy page, product pages, FAQ and footer badges should all state the same window.

## Contact options

A shopper with one unanswered question has two choices: ask or leave. Whether they ask depends on how easy you make it. On each of the 107 homepages I recorded every way a shopper could reach the brand: a chat or help desk widget, a clickable email link, or a clickable phone number.

- **60% load a chat or help desk widget.** 64 of 107.
- **24% link an email address.** 26 of 107.
- **12% link a phone number.** 13 of 107.
- **32% offer none of the three on the homepage.** 34 of 107. A shopper has to find a contact page.

| Contact option on the homepage | Stores | Share of 107 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chat or help desk widget | 64 | 60% |
| Clickable email link | 26 | 24% |
| Clickable phone link | 13 | 12% |
| Email or phone link | 29 | 27% |
| None of the three | 34 | 32% |

| Widget detected | Stores |
| --- | --- |
| Gorgias | 35 |
| Shopify Inbox | 20 |
| Zendesk | 5 |
| Kustomer | 4 |
| Chatbot or AI agent tools | 3 |
| Re:amaze | 2 |
| Richpanel | 2 |
| Intercom, Tidio, Gladly | 1 each |

Some stores load more than one. A widget loading doesn't mean it's staffed or visible on mobile, only that the store has set one up.

Stores have moved contact into chat widgets and away from email and phone. That works when the chat answers quickly. It fails when the widget opens a form that promises a reply "within 24 to 48 hours", which is just email with extra steps. For a $30 product, that's usually fine. For a $300 product, a mattress, a supplement with a health question behind it, or a gift that has to arrive by a date, the shopper who can't get an answer in the moment buys somewhere they can.

AI agents shopping on a buyer's behalf don't use your chat. Shopify's managed `agents.md` file deliberately leaves out contact details and points agents to your policies and product data instead ([Shopify](https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/themes/architecture/templates/agents-md-liquid)). So the questions your support team answers most often should be answered on your product pages, FAQ and policies too, where both shoppers and AI can read them. See [Shopify FAQ pages](/reports/shopify-faq-pages-ai-search-2026).

### What to do with your contact options

1. **Look at your chat logs before you change anything.** The top ten questions tell you what your product pages are missing.
2. **Answer those questions on the page.** Every answer you add to a product page is a chat you don't need, and a fact AI can repeat.
3. **Make the widget honest.** If no one replies live, say when they will. "We reply within 2 hours, 9 to 5 Pacific" beats a chat bubble that goes silent.
4. **Show a phone number if your price justifies it.** For high consideration products, test showing it on product pages and in the cart, measured by conversion rate.
5. **Check it on mobile.** Make sure the chat bubble doesn't cover Add to Cart or the sticky checkout button.

## Back in stock alerts

A sold out product page is a shopper who already wanted to buy. Either you capture them, with a "notify me" form that brings them back when stock returns, or they buy the same thing somewhere else. I checked the full catalog of each store for products with every variant sold out, then opened one sold out product page per store and looked for a back in stock signup.

- **Most stores have something sold out.** 77 of 105 catalogs (73%) had at least one fully sold out product.
- **It's a small share of the catalog.** The median store had 4% of products sold out.
- **36% of sold out pages capture nothing.** 28 of the 77 pages I checked had no way to get notified.
- **64% offered a signup.** 49 of 77.

| Product area | Sold out pages with a signup |
| --- | --- |
| Beauty | 10 of 13 |
| Supplements | 7 of 11 |
| Apparel | 9 of 14 |
| Food and drink | 8 of 13 |
| Pet | 6 of 10 |
| Home | 9 of 16 |

The samples per area are small, so treat these as a direction, not a ranking. Home and pet stores were the least likely to capture the shopper, and beauty the most likely. Even the best area left about a quarter of sold out pages with nothing.

On the 49 pages with a signup, the most common setups were Klaviyo's back in stock feature and dedicated back in stock apps, with a few wishlist apps that double as alerts. I didn't compare apps, but the setups that work best share three things: the form appears on the exact sold out variant, it works on mobile, and the email goes out the day stock returns, not days later.

A sold out product is often a best seller, which is why it sold out. Its page keeps getting traffic from search, ads, emails and AI answers that recommended it. Without a signup, every one of those visits ends there. A back in stock list also tells you which products to reorder first and in what depth.

### What to do with sold out pages

1. **Check every sold out page, not just one.** Variants matter: a sold out size on an otherwise available product needs the form too.
2. **Turn it on.** If you use Klaviyo, its back in stock feature works with most themes. Otherwise use a dedicated app.
3. **Make the button obvious.** "Notify me when it's back" in place of the greyed out Add to Cart, not a link at the bottom.
4. **Offer an alternative.** Show a similar in stock product right below, for the shopper who can't wait.
5. **Keep sold out pages live if the product is coming back.** Hide or redirect only the ones that are gone for good.
6. **Watch the list size.** It's the cleanest demand signal you have for planning stock.

## Announcement bars

The announcement bar is the first line of text on almost every page of a Shopify store. It's seen more than any headline, product description or banner, and most stores fill it with whatever the last promotion was. I read the announcement bar on each of the 107 homepages. 63 showed readable text.

- **Free shipping dominates.** 35 of 63 bars (56%) promoted free shipping, almost always with a threshold.
- **Sales come second.** 22 of 63 (35%) promoted a sale or discount, 12 of them for Black Friday in mid November.
- **The trust messages are missing.** Only 2 bars mentioned returns. Only 3 used social proof like review counts.
- **A quarter of bars stack offers.** 25 of 63 crammed several messages into one line or rotated between them.
- **44 of 107 homepages had no readable message** when the page loaded, either no bar or an empty one.

| Message | Stores | Share of 63 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free shipping (with or without a threshold) | 35 | 56% |
| Sale, percent off or bundle savings | 22 | 35% |
| Black Friday or Cyber Monday | 12 | 19% |
| New product or limited edition | 11 | 17% |
| Free gift or sample with purchase | 7 | 11% |
| Holiday shop or gift sets | 7 | 11% |
| Discount code to type | 5 | 8% |
| Social proof (reviews, customers, rankings) | 3 | 5% |
| Pay later, HSA or FSA | 3 | 5% |
| Returns or guarantee | 2 | 3% |
| Subscription offer | 2 | 3% |

Messages can fall in more than one row.

When more than half of stores use the bar for free shipping, the message stops working as a reason to choose you. Free shipping still belongs somewhere visible, but it's become the price of entry, not an edge. The messages that answer a shopper's real hesitation, "what if it doesn't work for me?" and "is this brand legit?", were almost absent. None used a delivery cutoff like "order by 2pm, ships today", which is one of the most persuasive messages during the holidays.

On a phone, a single line that reads "free shipping over $50 | 20% off sitewide | new flavors | refer a friend" is cut off after the first item or two. A rotating bar is worse in a different way: each message shows for a few seconds, and the shopper usually sees only the first before they scroll. The median message was 68 characters, which fits on most phones. Stacked bars ran much longer.

### What to do with your announcement bar

1. **Pick one job for the bar.** The message most likely to remove a reason not to buy, shown first.
2. **Test a trust message against free shipping.** "60 day money back guarantee" or "Free returns" is rare enough to stand out. Run it as an A/B test, with conversion rate as the measure.
3. **Add a cutoff during gifting season.** "Order by Dec 18 for Christmas delivery" answers the question every December shopper has.
4. **Show progress, not just the threshold.** In the cart, "You're $12 away from free shipping" does more than the bar ever will.
5. **One or two messages, not five.** If you rotate, slow it down and put the important one first.
6. **Remove old promotions the day they end.** An expired code in the bar costs trust.

## Why this matters for AI search too

AI shopping agents now read store policies directly. Shopify's managed `agents.md` file points shopping agents to each store's published policies ([Shopify](https://shopify.dev/docs/storefronts/themes/architecture/templates/agents-md-liquid)). In a 2026 test of 122 Shopify stores by Search Engine Journal, agents handled return windows well, but no store gave a usable answer about shipping cost to a specific city, and cancellations mostly failed ([source](https://searchenginejournal.com/your-store-is-agent-ready-by-default-we-tested-122-shopify-stores/591767)).

That means the exact words in your policies are what an AI repeats to a shopper. A policy that never states a number of days, or buries the window in the fourth paragraph, gives AI nothing clear to say. See [every Shopify store now has an agents.md file](/reports/shopify-agents-md-ai-agents-2026) and [shipping and return policy schema](/reports/shopify-shipping-return-policy-schema-2026).

Shipping, returns, contact options, sold out pages and the rest of what shoppers check before buying are part of my [Full Store Audit](/full-store-audit).

## How this was done

- **Stores:** 107 Shopify brand stores, 17 to 18 in each of six product areas (supplements, beauty, food and drink, pet, home, apparel), drawn at random from my dataset of 620 Shopify brand stores. Client stores were excluded. The same 107 stores were used for all five checks.
- **Shipping policies:** each store's published policy at `/policies/shipping-policy`, fetched November 16, 2026. 95 had a readable policy. Elements were detected from the policy text with keyword rules (for example "cutoff", "before 2pm", "tracking", "processing"), with day ranges read as "X to Y days", and spot checked by hand.
- **Return policies:** each store's published policy at `/policies/refund-policy`, fetched November 16, 2026. 101 stores had a readable policy with more than a few lines of text. The return window is the longest number of days between 7 and 120 stated in the policy. Elements were detected with keyword rules (for example "final sale", "non refundable", "restocking fee", "prepaid label") and spot checked by hand.
- **Contact options:** each homepage loaded on November 16, 2026 in a desktop browser. Email and phone counted when the page had a `mailto:` or `tel:` link. Chat and help desk tools counted when the tool's script loaded on the page. HubSpot was excluded, because its script is mostly used for tracking.
- **Back in stock:** sold out products from each store's public product list (up to 250 products, gift cards excluded). A product counted as sold out when every variant was unavailable. 105 stores returned a product list. The first sold out product page per store was checked for a visible signup ("notify me", "email me when available", "join the waitlist" and similar) or the script of a known back in stock tool.
- **Announcement bars:** the text of the announcement bar element on each homepage, loaded in a desktop browser. Bars showing only cookie notices, accessibility prompts or a default "Welcome to our store" were excluded, leaving 63 with a readable message. Message types were classified with keyword rules and checked by hand. Free shipping thresholds in dollars were compared with each store's shipping policy.
- **Limits:** a store may state terms elsewhere, such as an FAQ, a help center, product pages or the cart, and contact details on other pages. This covers the published Shopify policy pages and the homepage. Some bars load or change after the page loads, or differ by country and device. This is one snapshot during the run up to Black Friday, so sale messages are higher than they would be in a quieter month. Back in stock covers one sold out page per store.

No store or brand is named in this report.
